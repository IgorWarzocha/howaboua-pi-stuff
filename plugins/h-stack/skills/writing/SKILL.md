Write with an editor's ear and a mechanic's clarity. Preserve the author's voice, stance, humour, and strength of claim.

## Prose

Give the piece a real point and write for its audience and venue. An email, essay, product page, manual, and story need different voices. Ground specificity in supplied context and verified facts. Keep facts, opinions, assumptions, uncertainty, and creative invention distinct.

Fix the thought before polishing vocabulary. Diagnose missing substance, unsupported claims, flattened voice, wrong genre, or mechanical structure. Cut ceremonial openings and closers, canned empathy, fake momentum, inflated stakes, and conclusions that merely repeat the piece.

Avoid automatic "this, not that" contrasts, forced groups of three, self-answered rhetorical questions, and headings that only label the next heading. Let structure follow the material. Name the actor, action, mechanism, or result instead of describing abstract value.

Keep warmth, polish, technical vocabulary, and deliberate oddness when they carry meaning. Artificial fragments, slang, profanity, and fake jokes do not make prose human. Preserve a memorable phrase at its strongest use rather than repeating it into a catchphrase.

When asked to write or rewrite, lead with the finished prose. For diagnosis, explain the important pattern and quote only enough to make it inspectable. Stop polishing before the author's voice disappears.

For interface copy, assume a capable user. Include technical detail when it helps them judge progress, choose, or recover. Do not explain elementary concepts or obvious controls, defend implementation choices, or repeat disclaimers. Make the copy belong to the product, not an engineering report.

When addressing an agent, use "you" for the reader, "the user" for the person in the active conversation, and "users" for people using what's built or documented. Prefer precise roles such as caller, maintainer, or reviewer when they matter.

## Telegraphic prose

Telegraphic style drops recoverable words such as articles, pronouns, and auxiliaries. "Checkpoint prose" is shorthand for this style in operational writing, not a type of handoff. Use sparse, topic-led fragments. Cut repeated subjects and scaffolding. Keep exact names, conditions, exceptions, and recovery. Use arrows for real transitions. Do not just shorten full sentences or squeeze out spaces.

Before: "If the service fails to start after the change, restore the previous configuration and report the failing check. Do not restart it a second time."

Telegraphic: "Startup fails after change → restore prior config, report failing check. No second restart."

When delegating, say "Use telegraphic prose" and include the example if the worker cannot load a writing skill. For token-saving work, compare before and after with the same tokenizer.

## Punctuation

Follow the author's or venue's spelling conventions. If none are set, prefer British spelling.

Use periods, commas, question marks, and occasional exclamation marks. Use straight apostrophes and quotation marks. Colons introduce real lists, examples, or labels.

Do not use em dashes, en dashes, semicolons, ellipsis glyphs, decorative three-dot ellipses, or spaced hyphens as dash substitutes. Avoid parenthetical asides and slash constructions such as `and/or`.

Give each sentence one main thought, but keep conditions with their consequences. Keep a longer sentence when its ideas belong together. Make pronouns unambiguous.

Preserve exact punctuation in code, commands, paths, URLs, identifiers, and quoted source.
